History of Computers
​ A Computer is basically any device used to compute. There is computer hardware made for specific purposes like performing calculations, etc. and then there are general purpose computers that can be re-purposed to do different tasks by anyone, for example our laptops, desktops, etc. However the first time the term “Computer” appeared it was used to describe a job title of actual people who were paid to perform computations.
​ The first computing devices are were invented when people set out to solve problems that surpassed the computing ability of their minds. There is history of Computing devices that goes back thousands of years. The earliest recorded computing devices are tally sticks like the Ishango bone.
​ These tally sticks were used mostly for mnemonic purposes and basic but lengthy calculations. The Ishango bone was used around 19,000 BC which was the Old Stone Age. A relatively-modern example of this type of technology can be imagined as prayer beads, and electronic counters.
​ There are are records of Ancient computing devices like the Antikythera mechanism which was used to predict astronomical positions. In the picture we can see a part of this device. It was discovered by Greek archaeologist Valerios Stais in 1901 among the wreckage retrieved near Antikythera island.
​ The instrument is believed to have been designed by Greek scientists, and is acknowledged as the first known analogue computer. Its complexity suggests that it must have predecessors that built up to this. It is determined to be made around 87 BC.
​ However when it comes to ancient computers we can’t forget the well known Chinese abacus aka “The Suanpan”. First appeared in a 190 CE book of Eastern Han Dynasty called “Supplementary Notes on the Art of Figures” by Xu Yue”.
While the exact design of this device is not known, this device could be used to perform both decimal and hexadecimal computations.
​ Different versions of the Abacus were used around the world, the most famous versions being the Suanpan, and the Roman hand abacus.
​ Then there was The Pascaline aka Pascal’s calculator invented by French Mathematician Blaise Pascal.
​ This machine was able to perform addition of two numbers, and multiplication, division through repeated addition and subtraction.
​ The Pascaline was famous for its ability to carry leftover to next units.
​ This machine was designed in 1642.
​ Later in 1672 German mathematician Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz famously known as the sort of a co-inventor of calculus developed a device called “The Stepped Reckoner”.
​ In this image we can see the replace of the Stepped Reckoner. This was the first calculator that could perform all the arithmetic calculations. The machine was beyond the technology of its time and had mechanical problems in addition to a significant design flaw that rendered it unable to perform reliably.
​ Then in 1851 came the Arithmomètre, the first digital mechanical calculator strong and reliable enough to be used offices.
​ This calculator could add and subtract two numbers directly and could perform long multiplications and divisions effectively.
​ A man named Charles Xavier Thomas de Colmar patented and manufactured this design from 1851 to 1915.
​ It was the first commercially successful mechanical calculator.
​ With the launch of the Arithmomètre the mechanical calculator industry came to be; and for forty years, from 1851 to 1890, the Arithmomètre was the only type of mechanical calculator in commercial production.
​ Then came the revolutionary “Punch card” era. From the invention of computer programming languages up to the mid-1970s, most computer programmers created, edited and stored their programs/software on punched cards. A typing error resulted in throwing away the punch card and redoing all the work again.
​ The punch cards were a digital mechanical technology, like all mechanical tech it was subject to wear and tear. This technology was applied on tape, paper, wood, metal sheets, etc. and was very successful. The technology is so practical that it is used even today in many different devices, mostly antique replicas and factories.
​ However here is the important stuff, The punch card was probably the first medium to store digital data for Electronic computation purposes. Punch cards were used in looms, calculators, and musical instruments like those piano’s with built-in scrolls that can play music without a player, you know the ones we saw on “Tom and Jerry”.
​ In 1819 an English Mathematician named Charles Babbage started the construction of a device called “The Difference Engine”, which was a device made to work with polynomial functions (remember algebra I).
​ The device was completed in 1822, and Babbage made his invention public on the 14th of June, 1822.
​ Believe it or not but the Difference Engine was in fact closely related to the Antikythera mechanism in a manner of speaking.
​ Then in 1837 Charles Babbage proposed a successor to this device and called it “The Analytical Engine”. It was a mechanical general-purpose computer, like the modern computers we use today. It had an ALU aka arithmetic logic unit, a control flow in the form of conditional branching and loops, and integrated memory, in other words it was in fact an ancestor of the modern computer.
​ After all this went down with Babbage a string of other devices came to the scene, like the following ones:
​ There was the “Mallock Machine”, built by Rawlyn Richard Manconchy Mallock of Cambridge University in 1933. It was an electrical analogue computer used to solve simultaneous linear differential equations.
​ Then there was the “Water Integrator” built in the soviet union in 1936 by Vladimir Lukyanov. It could solve (partial) differential equations. It have chambers where water levels represented stored numbers, and the rate of flow between them represented mathematical operations.
​ There was also the “Model K”, built in 1936 a scientist named George Robert Stibitz from Bell Labs. It was the first electric digital computer.
​ There was the “Z1” computer, a mechanical device designed and built by German scientist Konrad Zuse around 1936 to 1938. It was a binary electrically driven mechanical calculator with limited programmability.
​ Then from 1930’s to 1940’s there was a wave of computers called “The Differential Analysers”, they were basically mechanical analogue computers designed to solve differential equations by integration, using wheel-and-disc mechanisms to perform the integration.
​ In WWII an English telephone engineer Thomas Harold Flowers from the British Post Office designed and built the “Colossus” computer at Bletchley Park park; the world's first programmable electronic computer. It’s purpose was to help solve encrypted German messages. A similar thing was done earlier at Bletchley Park by Alan Turing when he created an electro-mechanical device to rival the Nazi Enigma Machine.
​ Alan Turing is widely considered the father of theoretical computer science, and AI. Much of Turing’s work was either classified or deep-sixed by the British government under the Official Secrets Act.
​ ENIAC was the first electronic general-purpose computer, designed by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert for military purposes in 1945.
​ The Manchester Baby aka The Small-Scale Experimental Machine (SSEM) was the world's first electronic computer that could store its programs inside electronic memory. It was built at the University of Manchester, UK, by Frederic C. Williams, Tom Kilburn, and Geoff Tootill, and ran its first program on 21 June 1948.
​ Another notable invention was the “Monetary National Income Analogue Computer” aka MONIAC aka “The Phillips Hydraulic Computer” or the “Financephalograph”. It was created in 1949 by the New Zealand economist William Phillips.
​ In 1948 a device called “Curta” came to markets. It was a mechanical pocket calculator developed by Austrian engineer Curt Herzstark. It was basically the lovechild of the Stepped Reckoner and the Arithmomètre. These devices were replaced by electronic calculators around 1970s.
​ The Pegasus was an early British vacuum tube based computer built by Ferranti, Ltd. an electrical engineering and equipment firm that existed form 1885 to 1993.
​ The “Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Automatic Computer” aka The CSIRAC or CSIR MK 1 was the first digital computer in Australia. It was developed by an Australian team led by Trevor Pearcey and Maston Beard. It was released in 1949. It is also the oldest surviving first-generation electronic computer in history, and was the first computer in the world to play digital music.
​ In 1953 IBM developed a rival to UNIVAC named “The IBM 702”, which was the first computer to use magnetic tapes.
​ In 1953 we saw the IBM 650, the first mass produced computer in the world. It was produced from 1953 to 1963.
In 1956 we saw the IBM 305, which was the first commercial computer that used a moving-head hard disk drive and could store five  million characters of accounting data, which was equivalent of 6400 punch cards or 5MB of data.
The SAGE computer was also developed by IBM around this time, and to this day is the largest, heaviest, and most expensive computer ever built. It was built for the USAF by Jay Forrester and George Valley.
​ In 1976 at Los Alamos National Laboratory the first Cray-1 super computer was installed. Cray-1 was developed by Cray Research, under Seymour Cray and Lester Davis. It was the most successful super computer in history.
​ Then on April 11, 1976 “Apple I” was released by Apple. It was designed and hand-made by Steve “Woz” Wozniak. It was sold at $666.66 USD at the time.
​ After observing Apple’s success IBM released its own line of personal computers, the first model was IBM 5150 released on 12th August, 1981. The design team was led by Don Estridge.
​ In the Early 2000’s computers were everywhere! in schools, homes, offices, etc. In the modern world computers have become a consumer driven market with a need for a laptop, smartphone, a gaming console, etc. for each individual. Computers these days are less about need, and more about keeping up with the trends.

​ When naming variables in JavaScript make sure you follow the following rules:
1. The name must not have spaces.
2. The first character must be an alphabet, or underscore.
3. Always use meaningful names so you can later verify which variable does what.
4. Use camelCasing where the fist character must be a small alphabet, and if the variable named is comprised of multiple words attached together then capitalize the first alphabet of each word. So “First Name” becomes “firstName”, “Last Name” becomes “lastName”, etc.
5. Variable names are case-sensitive.

1. Sting : which is anything wrapped in symbols like single quotation, double quotation, or back ticks.

​ For example: 1, 2, or 3.1415.
3. Boolean : which is either true or false but not a string.
4. Null or Undefined : which are both distinguished data-types meaning a null value or an absence of value.

Strongly typed:- Means it is hard for data-types to interact with each other, and convert to one another.
Weakly typed:- Means it’s easy to switch among data-types, and make them interact.

Optional & Default parameters
​ In simple JavaScript every parameter is optional. However that is not true in TypeScript. In TypeScript if we want optional parameters than we have use the “?” after the parameter name. For example:
TypeScript code:
function myFunk ( a , b ? ) {
  return a + b ;
}
console . log ( myFunk ( 1 , 2 )); //logs : 3
console . log ( myFunk ( 1 )); //logs : NaN
​ Here on the 1st line the parameter “b” is optional since it has the “?” question mark symbol as a suffix. Notice how when we don’t pass this parameter an argument we get a “NaN” value. That is because the default value of b was “undefined” when we tried to sum up “1” and “undefined”. This can be avoided with default values:
TypeScript code:
function myFunk ( a , b = 0 ) {
  return a + b ;
}
console . log ( myFunk ( 1 , 2 )); //logs : 3
console . log ( myFunk ( 1 )); //logs : 1
​ Notice on the 1st line we have the default value of the “b” parameter set to “0”, this way the parameter “b” is optional, if we don’t pass an argument to it the function will use the default value instead.

Class based OOP
​ TypeScript enables us to use Class based Object Oriented Programming. Classes are basically Object factories and have been introduced in ES6, and TypeScript combines it with express compiling and static data-typing to bring us a lot of exciting stuff. Classes have a fundamental structure which goes like this:
1. On the first few lines inside our Class's body we declare properties/members for our Class’s Instances and predetermine their data-types.
2. After that we define a constructor() which takes in arguments ultimately used by the constructor() to initialize our properties with values.
3. Then we follow with methods for our Class’s Instances.
Look at the following code:
TypeScript code:
class Person {
     name : string ;
     constructor ( myName : string ) {
         this . name = myName ;
    }
     intro () {
         return "Hello, i am " + this . name ;
    }
}
let harry = new Person ( "Harry" );
let larry = new Person ( "Larry" );
let terry = new Person ( "Terry" );
console . log ( harry . intro ()); //logs: “Hello, i am Harry”
console . log ( larry . intro ()); //logs: “Hello, i am Larry”
console . log ( terry . intro ()); //logs: “Hello, i am Terry”
​ Notice how we created a single Class named “Person” which takes in a parameter named “myName” and assigned it’s value to the “name ” member of our Instances “Harry”, “Larry”, and “Terry”. Here “Harry”, “Larry”, and “Terry” are Instances of the “Person” Class .
​ The members of Instances like “Harry.name” are public by default. This means the data can leak out of our Instances. Look at the following example of our data in the member “name” leaking out:
TypeScript code:
class Person {
     name : string ;
     constructor ( myName : string ) {
         this . name = myName ;
    }
     intro () {
         return "Hello, i am " + this . name ;
    }
}
let harry = new Person ( "Harry" );
console . log ( harry . name ); //logs: “Harry”
​ See how we were able to see the name of the “Harry” instance without any special permissions? This not meant to be done like this, there is a better way. To avoid this we can also make these members private using the “private” modifier as a prefix to the variable declaration. Look at the following example:
TypeScript code:
class Person {
     private name : string ;
     constructor ( myName : string ) {
         this . name = myName ;
    }
     intro () {
         return "Hello, i am " + this . name ;
    }
}
let harry = new Person ( "Harry" );
console . log ( harry . name ); //throws error
​ Now since we have made our “name” member private it is not directly accessible. Now it is only accessible via the intro() method.
​ To access private members from our Instances we use special methods called “Accessors” aka “Getters” & “Setters”. Let’s see how to make them:
TypeScript code:
class Person {
     private _name : string ;
     private _age : number ;
     constructor ( myName : string , myAge : number ) {
         this . _name = myName ;
         this . _age = myAge ;
    }
     get name () : string {
       return this . _name ;
    }
     get age () : number {
       return this . _age ;
    }
     set name ( newName : string ) {
       this . _name = newName ;
    }
     set age ( newAge : number ) {
       this . _age = newAge ;
    }
}
let harry = new Person ( "Harry" , 32 );
console . log ( harry . name , harry . age ); //logs: Harry 32
harry . age = 33 ;
console . log ( harry . name , harry . age ); //logs: Harry 33

​Using extends, protected, and super()
​ Inheritance of properties is a pillar of OOP. Think of it as writing reusable blocks of code which are made up of other reusable blocks of code. There are a few ways to use it to our advantage with TypeScript. The first way we will look at is the extends feature that is used to extend the properties of one Class to another. Look at the following code:
TypeScript code:
class Animal {
  adopt () {
     console . log ( "You made a new friend" );
  }
}

  meow () {
     console . log ( "Meow Meow!" );
  }
}
const myCat = new Cat ();
myCat . adopt ();
myCat . meow ();
​ Notice how the class “Cat” is an extension of the class “Animal”. Also notice how the instance “myCat” is an instance of the class “Cat” but still is able to use the “adopt” method from the “Animal” class. This is the magic of extending classes!
​ Now let’s look at the protect modifier. TypeScript allows three basic modifiers to control data leaks, they are “public” where all properties are public by default, “private” which doesn’t allow data to leak without Accessors like “get” and “set”, and the third one is “protected” which is basically private but allows inheriting properties to extending classes. Look at the following code:
TypeScript code:
class Animal {
  protected name : string ;
  constructor ( name : string ) { this . name = name ;}
}
class Cat extends Animal {
   constructor ( name : string ){
     super ( name );
  }
  meow () {
    console . log ( `Meow Meow! My name is ${ this . name }` );
  }
}
const myCat = new Cat ( "Tom" );
myCat . meow ();
​ We can use the super() function to inherit properties from parent classes. Here we have use super() in the “Cat” class to accept the inheritance of the “name” property from the “Animal” class.

Interfaces
​ TypeScript gives us a simple way called Interfaces to create our own data-types. Lets look the how to create Interfaces:
TypeScript code:
interface MyDates {
  myWord : string ;
  myDigit : number ;
  myReason ?: string ;
}
let myFavDate : MyDates = {
  myWord : "Sep 28" ,
  myDigit : 928 ,
  myReason : "no reason" ,
}
console . log ( myFavDate ); //logs: { myWord: 'Sep 28', myDigit: 928, myReason: 'no reason' }
​ See how we created a new data-type named “myDates”, any variables that belong to this data-type must follow the following rules:
1. It must be objects that have two properties named “myWord”, and “myDigit”.
2. It may or may not have a property named “myReason”. (its optional because of the “?” operator as a suffix)
3. The “myWord” property must be a string.
4. The “myDigit” property must be a number.
5. The “myReason” property (if it exists) must be a string.
​ There is also a modifier named “readonly” which is like the keyword “const” but for object properties. It creates immutable properties for objects. Lets look at how to use it:
TypeScript code:
interface MyDates {
  myWord : string ;
  readonly myDigit : number ;
}
let myFavDate : MyDates = {
  myWord : "Sep 28" ,
  myDigit : 928 ,
}
myFavDate . myWord = "28 Sep" ;
myFavDate . myDigit = 289 ; //throws error
​ This code will work fine up until the final line, on the final line we attempt to modify “myDigit” which is a readonly property, so the compiler will throw the following error “Cannot assign to 'myDigit' because it is a read-only property”.
​ By this time you must have realized how powerful interfaces are, imagine use interfaces within interfaces like the following code:
TypeScript code:
interface Weapon {
  readonly type : string ;
  readonly madeof ?: string ;
}
interface Hero {
  readonly name : string ;
  lvl : number ;
  weapon : Weapon ;
}
let myPlayer : Hero = {
  name : "Ray" ,
  lvl : 14 ,
  weapon : {
     type : "sword" ,
     madeof : "steel"
  }
}
console . log ( myPlayer );
/* logs:
{ name: 'Ray',
  lvl: 14,
  weapon: { type: 'sword', madeof: 'steel' } }
*/
​ See how we have use the interface named “Weapon” inside the interface “Hero”? Never thought it would be this easy in JavaScript. Before TypeScript if we wanted to write a piece of code like this we didn’t had the option of this type of strict data-typing and inheritance, at best what we could do was to keep track of data-types and inheritance ourselves.

TypeScript modules
​ Modules are containers of reusable code. They are an essential part of building software that scales without disruption. In TypeScript we have two types of modules:
1. Internal modules aka Namespaces .
2. External modules aka Modules.
​ There are two ways to write Internal modules: “unnamed” aka “Implicit” modules, and “Named” modules aka “Namespaces”. Implicit code is code that is written inside the source-code file, and is not part of a named module, this is considered a bad practice in general; whenever we can we should isolate code into named modules. Lets look at the following code:
TypeScript code:
namespace ModuleName {
  //write code here
}
​ This is a named module, declared with the keyword “namespace”, and named with the first letter capitalized and the rest camel-cased. The important thing to remember is that nothing leaks outside of this module unless deliberately “exported” by the programmer.
​ Let’s look at an example:
TypeScript code:
namespace Zoo {
  export class Animal {
     name : string ;
    constructor ( myName : string ) { this . name = myName ;}
  }
}
const myCat = new Zoo . Animal ( "Jacob" );
console . log ( myCat ); //logs: Animal { name: 'Jacob' }
​ In this code we have used the “namespace” keyword to declare an internal module named “Zoo”. Inside this module we have a class named “Animal” that takes in an argument and assigns it to a property named “name”. Then we have written a variable named “myCat” and made it an instance of the class “Animal” by extracting/importing “Animal” from “Zoo” using the dot-notation.
​ Essentially we have created a class and a single instance of it. There are two things to notice here, the first one is the “export” keyword used to export the class “Animal” outside of the “Zoo” module, the second thing to notice is the dot-notation used to access “Animal” from “Zoo” like “Zoo.Animal”.
​ This is the way to write internal modules aka namespaces. We can also use the “module” keyword instead of the “namespace” keyword but that practice is discouraged.
​ Now lets look at actual “Modules” aka “external modules”. Make a new TypeScript file right where your “index.ts” file is, and name it “myModule.ts”. Now add the following code to it:
TypeScript code for “myModule.ts”:
export let x = "Hello World" ;
and then add the following code in your “index.ts” file:
TypeScript code for “myModule.ts”:

console . log ( x ); //logs: Hello World
​ Now assuming your “tsconfig.json” file (with code shown in the section “Initializing a tsconfig.json file”) is in place you can just execute the command “tsc && node index.js ” or else you can execute the command “tsc index.ts && node index.js ”. Combined together this code will log “Hello World” to the console/Terminal.
​ Here we have exported a variable named “x” using the “export” keyword, however, we can export anything we want including functions, classes, etc.

Working with Window title bar
​ To modify a Window Title like we can take several approaches, following is a list:
  • To set a Window-title, give a `title` property in the BrowserWindow object, and give it a string value, for example:
    new BrowserWindow({ height:800, width:600, title: ‘My Title’})
  • Don’t add a `title` element (<title></title> ) in our HTML pages, this way the “Window-title” will be the `name ` property from our app’s `package.json ` file.
  • Add a `title` element in our HTML pages, and the text inside that element will become our Window-title.
  • If both a `title` element and a `name` property is missing, then the default Window-title will be `Electron`.
  • To hide the Window-title-bar, give a `frame` property in the BrowserWindow object, and set it to `false `, for example:
    new BrowserWindow({ height:800, width:600, frame: false})
  • Use the following code to do something similar; you can set titleBarStyle to `hidden`, `hiddenInset`, etc. and experiment!
    new BrowserWindow({ height:800, width:600, titleBarStyle: ‘hiddenInset’ })
​ To make frameless windows drag-able without the title-bar use:
<body style=”-webkit-app-region: drag” >
​ This allows you to drag windows by holding window contents and dragging them.

IPC
​ Inter-process Communication AKA IPC is an Event Emitter module that allows the main and renderer processes of your software to send/receive messages. This communication can happen both synchronously and asynchronously.
​ The module has two parts, one for the main process called ipcMain , and one for the renderer process called ipcRenderer .
​ For the sake of simplicity , let’s assume the following basic definitions:
  • Process : A Process is a Javascript file in execution. For example main.js , index.js , etc. The difference between a file and a process is that a file also becomes a process when it’s code is being executed/ran.
  • Main Process : It’s usually referring to the main.js file, or whatever file we are using as an entry point in our package.json.
  • Renderer Process : It’s usually referring to files that are attached to our HTML files, like renderer.js in the electron-quick-start approach.
  • Preload File: This is a Javascript file that is always loaded with every web-page by default, without mentioning it in their HTML file. Since we’re keeping nodeIntegration false in our apps, our HTML files won’t have NodeJS and Electron’s features, to overcome this in a safe way, we will use a preload.js file to expose only certain features of NodeJS and Electron to our HTML and other front-end files. If you are using the electron-quick-start boilerplate, then you should have the renderer.js , main.js , and preload.js , created and attached by default.

  4. send-btn : This is just a unique element ID for our send button, and follows the standard HTML naming conventions.
  5. msg : This is also a unique element ID for our reply message box.
  6. arg : Found in renderer.js at line #8 and #9, this is just a parameter name (can be any word), this parameter stores the message we got from the reply.

TensorFlowJS
​ In it's essence TensorFlow and all other Machine Learning libraries are just collection of function and routines for High Tech Mathematics. It powers some of the greatest AI tech we have these days.
​ In recent chapters we have learned some Mathematics related with Tensors. As a revision remember the following terms:
  1. 4-dimensional Tensors are Arrays with 3-dimensional Tensors as members.
  2. 3-dimensional Tensors are Arrays with 2-dimensional Tensors as members.
  3. 2-dimensional Tensors aka Matrices are Arrays with 1-dimensional Tensors as members.
  4. 1-dimensional Tensors aka Arrays aka Vectors are Arrays with 0-dimensional Tensors as members.
  5. 0-dimensional Tensors aka Scalars are Numbers.
​ In Mathematics Tensors can have any amount of dimensions as we want, they have shape which is defined by the length of their members and sub-members, and sub-members of sub-members, and so on.
​ Just like Arrays, Tensors have index values too! However they look more like map coordinates, so a 1-dimensional Tensor aka Array have index values like 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, etc. a 2-dimensional Tensor has index values like (0, 0), (0 1), (0, 2), (1, 0), (1, 1), etc. and a 3-dimensional Tensor has index values like (0, 0, 0), (0, 0, 1), (1, 0, 1), (3, 1, 0), etc. and so on, more the  dimensions the more members present in an index position.
Note: Tensors, Arrays, Matrices, Vectors, etc. are not synonymous. They have different definitions in Mathematics, and Computer Programming. Don’t confuse them with each other! In TensorFlow Tensors are an immutable from of data.
